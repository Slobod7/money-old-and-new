In August 2019, Jyske Bank in Denmark offered a mortgage with a stated interest rate of −0.5%, fixed for up to ten years. Borrowers still paid fees, so the loan wasn’t free. But the number on the contract was below zero.

I have a degree in finance and banking, and I spent a year in a bank. I could have told you how to price that loan. I couldn’t have told you whether it was a problem, or for whom. That’s why I picked up Edward Chancellor’s The Price of Time.

His answer is that it was a very big problem. Interest, he argues, is the price of time. It decides which projects get built, which debts get taken on and which risks look worth taking. Hold that price too low for too long, and the economy rebuilds itself around a bargain that can’t last.

The UK edition came out on 7 July 2022. Two weeks later the ECB raised rates for the first time in 11 years, ending eight years below zero. So a book about what cheap money builds arrived just as cheap money ended. Four years on, we have a pension crisis, a bank failure and a stock market that got expensive again with rates far higher. I read the 2023 UK paperback, which adds a preface with a forecast. That can be checked too.

The last of those is the part I most wanted to work out. In September, Chancellor himself wrote a column headlined “The Everything Bubble is back and bigger than ever”. That’s awkward for a book that blames bubbles on cheap money, because money isn’t obviously cheap any more.

My verdict is read it, with reservations. He’s right about what breaks. He’s less right about when. And he leaves the biggest question open: what would higher rates have cost?

The short version

  • What holds up: his account of the risks cheap money hides. The UK gilt crisis of 2022 and the fall of Silicon Valley Bank in 2023 fit it closely.
  • What doesn’t: his forecast of prolonged inflation with negative real rates hasn’t come true in the US, and the case that higher rates would have left us better off is never really made.
  • The puzzle: US shares are near dot-com valuations with real yields at 2.86%. By his own yardstick, money has gone from clearly cheap to roughly fair, and the boom came back anyway.
  • The idea to keep: when something is cheap, ask what’s been built that only works at this price.

Who it’s for, and who should skip it

Read it if you follow rate decisions and want to know why they matter beyond your mortgage. Part One is history: Babylonian loans, the long fight over usury, John Law’s boom and bust in France. Part Two is the case against the years after 2008: zombie companies, financial engineering, the “everything bubble”, pensions and inequality. Part Three turns to emerging markets and China.

Skip it if you want a neutral guide to monetary policy, or a list of things to buy. This is a prosecution, not a survey. It won the Hayek Book Prize in 2023, and its conclusion is called “The New Road to Serfdom”, which tells you where it’s heading.

Short of time? Read the introduction, chapters 10 to 14 (zombies, financial engineering, the bubble, pensions, inequality) and the conclusion.

The argument in 150 words

Interest is the price of moving money through time. It tells borrowers what waiting costs and savers what waiting earns. Chancellor argues that after 2008 central banks held that price too low for too long. Cheap finance supported recovery, but also changed behaviour: weak firms could refinance, investors borrowed more, pension funds reached for yield, and distant promises became valuable. Rising asset prices rewarded existing owners. The resulting economy became dependent on easy money, making each attempt to withdraw it more dangerous. In this account, low rates did not simply reflect weak growth; they helped perpetuate it by directing capital towards the wrong uses. The warning is about a system, not just a stock-market bubble. Eventually the price of money would have to change, exposing arrangements that looked safe only while borrowing stayed cheap. The dispute is whether that diagnosis establishes what central banks should have done instead, and when.

Three terms help. A nominal rate is the number on the contract. A real rate takes inflation away: the ECB’s deposit rate is 2.50% and inflation was 4.0% in October, so the real rate is roughly −1.5%, as I worked through in my last piece, on the ECB. The natural rate is the real rate at which an economy neither overheats nor stalls. Nobody can observe it. Economists estimate it, and the ECB’s own discussion stresses how uncertain those estimates are. On page xxxi, Chancellor offers a rule of thumb: trend economic growth is a reasonable stand-in. That matters later.

What holds up

Risks that only show when the price changes

This is the book’s strongest ground. When safe bonds pay almost nothing, anyone who has promised a return has to find it somewhere else. They buy bonds that pay out further in the future, or they borrow to buy more. Longer bonds carry duration risk: the further off the payments, the harder a rise in yields hits the price today. None of it hurts while rates stay low, so it looks safe until the day it isn’t.

UK pensions, September 2022. Final-salary schemes had used borrowed money to hedge their long-term promises, a strategy called liability-driven investment. When gilt yields jumped after the government’s mini-budget, lenders demanded collateral. The Bank of England estimated more than £70 billion of margin and collateral calls between 23 September and 14 October. Schemes and funds sold about £37 billion of gilts over those weeks, which pushed yields higher and brought more calls. The Bank had to step in as a buyer to stop the spiral. The mini-budget set it off. What broke had been built in the low-rate years.

Silicon Valley Bank, March 2023. The bank had put a flood of new deposits into long-dated securities. Rising rates cut their market value, and its depositors turned out to be far quicker to leave than management had assumed. It failed on 10 March. The Federal Reserve’s own review also blames the bank’s management and its supervisors, so rates weren’t the only cause. They’re what exposed it.

The lesson is narrower than “rate rises break banks”. Assets that pay out over years, funded by money that can leave today, only work while nobody tests them.

There’s a twist, though: neither crisis spread. The Bank of England and the Fed stepped in, and the damage stopped. You can read that as proof the risks were manageable. I suspect Chancellor would read it as the rescue that feeds the next bubble. I’m with him on how these things break, and less sure than he is about how bad it gets.

The debt bill arrives slowly

Governments are the same story in slow motion. A state that borrowed for ten years at almost nothing doesn’t pay today’s rate until that debt matures, so the bill rises year by year, not overnight. That makes a budget look sturdy for longer than it is. It also means “debt is high” tells you where to look, not when anything breaks.

What doesn’t

The strongest objection: cause and effect may run the other way

Chancellor treats low rates as something central banks did. His critics say they’re something that happened to central banks. If households want to save more and companies want to invest less, the rate that balances the two falls, and central banks follow it down. Łukasz Rachel and Lawrence Summers make this case. If they’re right, holding rates higher after 2008 wouldn’t have restored discipline. It would have meant fewer jobs.

The argument isn’t settled. BIS researchers looking at a much longer history find that the usual saving and investment forces don’t explain real rates well, and that monetary regimes matter. Both sides can be partly right.

Martin Wolf’s 2023 essay sets the test for the whole case against easy money: say how high rates should have been, and what slump you’d have accepted to get there. Paschal Donohoe, reviewing the book as Ireland’s finance minister and president of the Eurogroup, enjoyed the history and still called it too much of a polemic: higher rates after the crisis, he argued, would have carried huge costs that the book doesn’t weigh.

I think they’re right, and it’s the book’s real weakness. Try the test on Europe. In June 2014, when the ECB first took its deposit rate below zero, euro area inflation was 0.5% and unemployment was 11.6%. If I’d had a vote that month, could I have argued for higher rates? I’d have struggled.

Zombies aren’t the same thing as bankruptcies

The zombie argument is the easiest to like: if the interest bill never forces a decision, money and workers stay stuck in businesses that should have closed. BIS research found that, on its broad definition, zombies’ share of listed firms in 14 advanced economies rose from about 2% in the late 1980s to 12% in 2016. But a 2021 Federal Reserve study found that US zombies were few, small and a minor share of business credit. And nothing shows that the firms that failed after rates rose were the zombies. More bankruptcies and a healthier economy aren’t the same thing.

Cheaper houses can still be harder to buy

This is the part I care about as a renter.

On a €200,000 loan over 25 years, the monthly payment is about €800 at 1.5% and €1,056 at 4%. Cut the loan by 15%, to €170,000, and the payment at 4% is still about €897. That’s a home 15% cheaper that costs 12% more a month. If cheap money priced people out, dearer money didn’t let them back in. Try your own numbers:

The 2026 puzzle: an expensive market without cheap money

In 2021 real yields were negative and everything was expensive, which is exactly his story. The US ten-year inflation-linked yield touched −1.19% that August. On 12 November 2026 it was 2.86%, and American shares are still very expensive. The usual gauge is the CAPE ratio: the price divided by ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. It was 39.95 on 13 November. The dot-com peak was 44.19. If cheap money makes bubbles, what’s making this one?

Chancellor has noticed. In his September column he argues that the five warning signs the strategist Richard Bernstein listed in 2021 are all flashing again: easy liquidity, leverage, crowds of retail investors, a rush of new share issues and heavy trading. He adds that the market is more concentrated this time. What isn’t back is the cheap money.

I can see three ways to read this:

  • Bubbles need a story as much as cheap money. AI is a good story. If so, his theory is incomplete.
  • Money is still cheap, if you measure it his way. This one can be tested.
  • The reckoning is just late. Perhaps. But nothing can ever disprove that.

In 2019 the answer was obvious. Yields sat far below growth, by 3.6 points in the euro area. Most of that gap has closed. Against the past year or the past decade, both swollen by the burst of inflation, yields are still a little below growth. Against growth before Covid, they’re level in the euro area and above it in the US. So on his own yardstick, money has gone from clearly cheap to roughly fair.

It’s cheaper at the short end: the Fed’s range of 3.75–4.00% and the ECB’s 2.50% are below growth on all three measures.

That was the most useful thing I took from writing this: “cheap” always needs a “compared with what?”

My read of the puzzle is mostly the first explanation, with a little of the second. Cheap money may be enough to make a bubble, but 2026 suggests it isn’t necessary. A strong story and easy credit can do it with ten-year yields near 5%. That’s a real hole in the book, because its main variable turns out not to be the only one.

The third reading is the one that bothers me. His columns keep saying the reckoning is on its way: “Economic hard landing is delayed but not cancelled” in May 2024, “Inflation’s not dead, just resting” that October, “The debt supercycle has reached its final leg” in July 2025. This January he helped Jeremy Grantham, his former colleague at GMO, write a memoir called The Making of a Permabear, so he knows how it looks. He may still turn out to be right, and early isn’t the same as wrong. But a forecast with no date can’t be wrong either, and that’s the problem.

The forecast

In the 2023 preface, on page xxi, Chancellor repeats the book’s expectation of “a prolonged bout of inflation and negative real interest rates”. Economists call that financial repression: rates held below inflation, so debts shrink at savers’ expense. It’s a real forecast, if an undated one.

  • United States: no. A ten-year real yield of 2.86% is the opposite of repression.
  • Euro area: patchy. The deposit rate moved above inflation in late 2023 and stayed there through 2024. It’s about 1.5 points below it again now, because of an energy shock and not by design.

Inflation itself did arrive, peaking at 9.1% in the US and 10.6% in the euro area in 2022. But the book came out that July, with the surge already under way, and Ben Bernanke and Olivier Blanchard put the first blame on energy, food and shortages, with tight labour markets coming later. Theirs is a US study, and Bernanke isn’t a neutral witness.

I’d call that partial credit for a warning, not full credit for a prediction.

The one idea worth keeping

It’s Frédéric Bastiat’s, really, and Chancellor opens the book with him: what is seen and what is not seen. A rate cut’s benefits are easy to see: jobs saved, loans refinanced, shares up. The costs turn up years later on someone else’s balance sheet, and nobody connects the two. You don’t have to accept the whole book to keep that.

I’ve turned it into one question I now ask of anything cheap: what’s been built that only works at this price? UK pension hedges only worked if gilt yields didn’t jump. SVB only worked if depositors stayed put. Plenty of budgets only work if debt can be rolled over cheaply. It’s a better question than “where will rates go?”, because you can actually answer it.

The same test cuts against him, too. The unseen cost of higher rates in 2014 would have been jobs that never existed, and you can’t see those either.

Through your wallet

Interest is the reward for waiting, and savers still aren’t getting much of it. In September the average euro area overnight deposit paid 0.30% and a new term deposit of up to a year paid 2.33%, the figures I used in the ECB piece. Inflation in October was 4.0%. Whatever the natural rate is, if your account pays less than inflation, your money is losing value while borrowers’ debts shrink.

I rent in Bucharest and my rent has been flat for two years, so dearer money hasn’t reached me through housing. My cash is in a savings account, in euros and dollars. I’m risk averse. I invest in the S&P 500, the housing market and a small percentage of Bitcoin, and a CAPE of 40 makes the first of those uncomfortable to read about. The book doesn’t tell me what to do about that, and it isn’t trying to. What it gave me is a better question to ask before I buy anything that looks cheap.

Read it with

Three books to set against it. Take them as pointers to the other side of the argument, not as reviews.

  • Ben Bernanke, 21st Century Monetary Policy (2022). The other side, from someone who made many of the decisions. Keep in mind that he’s defending his own record.
  • Liaquat Ahamed, Lords of Finance (2009). Central bankers and the gold standard on the way to the Depression. A reminder that money can do great damage by being too tight as well as too loose.
  • Robert Shiller, Narrative Economics (2019). How stories spread and move markets, which is the missing half of the 2026 puzzle.

For something shorter, John Shepperd’s review for the Society of Professional Economists likes the history and doubts that interest rates can explain as much as Chancellor wants them to.

Where I land

Was Chancellor right about cheap money? About what it builds, yes. The gilt crisis and SVB are what he described, and I understand them better for having read him. About when and how it ends, not so far: his forecast hasn’t happened in the biggest economy, and the boom he says is back returned when money wasn’t clearly cheap. About whether anyone could have done better, the book didn’t convince me, and that’s the question I most wanted answered.

Read it anyway. It’s the clearest case I’ve come across for treating an interest rate as a price rather than a setting, and you only see where the argument stops working once you’ve followed it.

What would change my mind? If something big breaks in government bonds or private lending at today’s rates, he deserves more credit than I’ve given him. If a CAPE near 40 and a real yield near 3% are still here in 2030 without a bust, the book explains the 2010s and not much else.
