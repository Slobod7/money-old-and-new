Every time someone in the euro area taps a card in a shop, there’s a good chance the payment runs through an American company. International card schemes, Visa and Mastercard, handle two-thirds of card transactions in the euro area, and 13 of the 21 euro countries have no card scheme of their own. That’s the problem the digital euro was designed to solve.

On the night of 18 November, negotiators for the European Parliament and the EU’s governments met for what Ireland, which chairs the talks for the member states, hoped would be the last round on the digital euro law. They broke up without a deal. They’re still split on when shops may refuse it, how the banks and payment firms that hand it out get paid, and who sets the holding limit, and two more rounds have been suggested, on 3 and 16 December. EU leaders asked in March for the law to be finished by the end of the year, so the ECB can run a pilot in 2027 and issue the first digital euros in 2029.

Listen to the ECB make its case and you hear two arguments. The first is about cards: Europe shouldn’t depend on foreign companies to pay for its groceries. The second is about stablecoins: if Europe doesn’t offer a digital euro, dollar tokens like Tether’s USDT and Circle’s USDC will fill the gap. A disclosure, since half this piece is about crypto: I write for Bitcoin.com and own some Bitcoin.

I think the first argument is strong and the second is a mistake. Not because dollar stablecoins don’t matter, but because the digital euro, as it’s being built, can’t compete with them, and the things that can are already happening. Sell it as the answer to both, and in five years it will be judged a failure at a job it was never designed to do.

The short version

  • The payments case is strong. Two-thirds of euro area card payments run on non-European schemes. A public payment option that every shop must accept fixes that, and only a public body can make it happen.
  • The stablecoin case is weak. The digital euro will pay no interest, have a holding limit, keep companies out and arrive in 2029. Dollar tokens are 99.4% of a $286 billion market, and they’re used mostly to trade crypto.
  • The stablecoin fight is elsewhere: in the EU’s crypto rules, in private euro tokens such as the 37-bank Qivalis, and in the ECB’s new system for settling tokenised trades.
  • The strongest objection: both fights meet at the till, so a public rail there defends against both. That’s partly right, and it’s still a payments argument.

As a payments project, the case is strong

Start with what the ECB says it’s for. In June, Piero Cipollone, the ECB board member in charge of the project, said “a digital euro will ensure that Europeans have a European public payment option”, and that it is designed “for domestic use first and foremost”. Read that way, it’s a payment network, not a new kind of savings.

The design follows. Basic use would be free for people. As legal tender, it would have to be accepted by shops, except small businesses and the self-employed who take no other electronic payments. Merchants would pay capped fees, and Parliament wants those fees to be no higher than for any other electronic payment. Offline payments would work phone to phone, with no record linking payer and payee. Building it will cost about €1.3 billion, and running it about €320 million a year from 2029, paid by the euro area’s central banks, the way they pay for banknotes.

Fees were one of the fights still open going into the last round. France opposes Parliament’s “no worse off” rule because it would compare digital euro fees with cheaper European payments too, and a source at the French finance ministry told Agence Europe that “the challenge is to make the digital euro competitive with non-European payment methods.” Notice that both sides of that argument are about Visa and Mastercard.

Then there’s the part nobody had to spell out five years ago. Negotiators are close to agreeing wording that would let people sanctioned by countries outside the EU use it, with judges of the International Criminal Court, sanctioned by the United States, in mind. Once a foreign government’s sanctions can reach a judge in The Hague, payments are a question of sovereignty, not just of fees.

This is the part I find convincing. Europe’s banks have tried for years to build their own network. Their latest attempt, Wero, has about 60 million users for payments between people, but it’s only starting in shops, in Belgium and Luxembourg, with France and Germany planned from 2027. That’s progress, but it’s five countries, after years of trying. A payment option that every shop in 21 countries must accept, and that any bank can put in its own app, is the kind of thing only a public body can make happen.

As a rival to dollar stablecoins, it’s built not to win

Now the second argument. In April 2025, Cipollone told the European Parliament that the measures taken by the new US Administration to promote dollar stablecoins “raise concerns”, and that a digital euro would “limit the potential for foreign currency stablecoins to become a common medium of exchange within the euro area.” Three months later, the US passed the GENIUS Act, which sets federal rules for dollar stablecoins and puts Washington behind them.

The worry is real. Look at the size of the two sides, though. On 20 November, DefiLlama counted $285.6 billion of stablecoins. 99.4% of that was tied to the dollar, led by USDT ($175 billion) and USDC ($69 billion). All the euro tokens together came to about €820 million, roughly 0.3%. The biggest, Circle’s EURC, was €502 million.

And look at what they’re used for. In the ECB’s own survey, 5% of euro area consumers held stablecoins in August 2025, mostly to trade crypto. Among people holding any crypto, 17% held USDT, 12% USDC and 10% EURC. The ECB’s economists conclude that dollar tokens “dominate euro area consumer exposure”. They found a hint of use for sending money abroad, too: people born outside the country were more likely to hold them.

Now set the digital euro against that. It will:

  • pay no interest, like a banknote;
  • have a holding limit per person. The figure isn’t set, but the ECB has modelled €3,000;
  • keep companies out, or nearly: the ECB has argued for a zero limit for businesses, to protect banks’ corporate deposits;
  • live on the Eurosystem’s own platform, not on the public blockchains where crypto is traded;
  • and arrive in 2029 at the earliest.

Every one of those is a sensible choice for something meant to replace a card. They protect banks from losing deposits, and they keep the ECB out of the business of running people’s savings. But together they rule it out of every place dollar stablecoins are actually used: crypto exchanges, lending protocols, company treasuries, money sent across borders. Nobody will trade Bitcoin against a capped, interest-free token on a central bank platform. Claiming it will hold back Tether is like claiming a new metro line will compete with airlines.

Where the stablecoin fight is actually being fought

None of this means Europe is doing nothing about dollar tokens. It’s doing three things, and all of them are moving faster than the digital euro.

1. The rules

The EU’s crypto law, MiCA, already did the job the ECB says it’s worried about. In January 2025, the EU’s markets regulator, ESMA, told exchanges to stop offering stablecoins that don’t meet MiCA’s rules, with a sell-only window until the end of March. Tether never applied for authorisation, so USDT went: Coinbase dropped it for European customers in December 2024 and Binance followed for its European spot markets. On 8 October this year, ESMA went further. Its opinion says crypto firms “should not provide crypto-asset services” for tokens that break MiCA at all.

USDC survived because Circle took a French e-money licence in July 2024. So the biggest dollar token still on regulated European exchanges is issued by a company supervised in Paris. MiCA also forbids paying interest on these tokens, which keeps them from competing with savings accounts.

2. Private euro tokens

Euro stablecoins are small but growing fast. A study by the data firm DIA found they grew 54% in the year to 19 October, while the supply of dollar tokens fell 1.3%.

The big test is Qivalis, owned by 37 European banks in 15 countries, including ING, UniCredit, BNP Paribas and BBVA. The Dutch central bank authorised it as an e-money institution on 14 October. Its token is to be backed one for one, with at least 40% in bank deposits and the rest in short-term euro area government bonds, and it wants to be on regulated exchanges from the first day. As of 20 November, there’s no token yet: it still has to publish the white paper MiCA requires. The company says it will launch later this year.

3. Central bank money for the tokenised world

On 21 September, the ECB launched Pontes, which lets banks and trading platforms settle trades in tokenised assets in central bank money, through the ECB’s existing payment system. It’s for professionals, not for you or me, and it’s already live: the ECB plans to settle its own purchases of tokenised securities through it. Central bank money for tokenised markets exists already. It just isn’t called the digital euro.

If I were writing the ECB’s speeches, this is where the stablecoin paragraphs would go. Rules that keep unregulated tokens off European exchanges, a bank-backed euro token that can be on those exchanges from day one, and central bank money that tokenised markets can settle in. That’s a real answer to Tether, and it exists now.

The strongest objection

Here’s the ECB’s case at its best, and it deserves more than a sentence.

Money follows payments. People hold the money they spend. If the easiest way to pay in an app, online or abroad becomes a dollar token, Europeans will start holding dollars. Then their savings follow, then prices, and the ECB loses grip on the money in its own economy. That’s how dollarisation happens, and it rarely starts with a decision. It starts with convenience.

The two fights are already merging. Visa began settling in USDC in the United States in December 2025, and cards that spend stablecoins already run on Visa and Mastercard. The company Europe wants to depend on less is plugging itself into the dollar tokens Europe worries about. Picture a European in 2030 paying at a supermarket with a Visa card that draws on a dollar stablecoin wallet. That’s one transaction, and it’s both fights at once.

Only public money is risk-free. A euro stablecoin, even one owned by 37 banks, is a promise by a company. If its reserves go wrong, holders queue up. The digital euro is a claim on the central bank, like a banknote. If Europe wants a digital euro that people trust without reading a white paper, a private token can’t provide it.

So, the ECB says, payments and stablecoins are one fight, not two, and whoever runs everyday payments ends up shaping the money.

My answer

The objection is right about where the two fights meet: the till. And that’s my point. At the till, the digital euro does one job, which is to replace a card. If dollar tokens ever reach European shops, they’ll come through a Visa or Mastercard card, and a European public rail every shop must take is the defence against both. That isn’t a stablecoin argument. It’s the Visa argument, made a second time.

Everywhere else dollar tokens are used, the digital euro won’t be there, by design: not on exchanges, not in company treasuries, not on the blockchains where tokenised markets are being built. There, the defence is MiCA, euro tokens like Qivalis, and Pontes.

On dollarisation: it happens where the local money fails. People reach for dollar tokens when their currency loses a large part of its value in a year and their banks can’t be trusted. Euro area inflation was 4.0% in October, which is uncomfortable, as I wrote in the ECB piece. It isn’t a currency collapse.

On risk-free money: agreed, and that’s a reason to build the digital euro. It isn’t a reason to say it will hold back Tether.

And overselling has costs. It sets a test the digital euro is bound to fail, since euro tokens will still be a fraction of dollar ones in 2030, and critics will say it didn’t work. It invites pressure to raise the holding limit to “compete”, which is exactly what banks fear. And it feeds the loudest fight, CBDC against crypto, when the project’s best support is a duller one: European payments for European shops. 169 MEPs voted against Parliament’s negotiating position in July. Arguments the ECB can’t win won’t bring any of them round.

We’ve been here before: eurodollars

Dollars held outside America, beyond American rules, aren’t new. The economic historian Catherine Schenk dates the eurodollar market to London in 1955, when Midland Bank began bidding for dollar deposits. By the 1960s, American limits on deposit interest were pushing more dollars offshore, and London banks were lending them all over the world.

Europe didn’t fight that market. London hosted it and grew rich on it. Dollar stablecoins are the same idea on a blockchain: dollars held and moved outside American banks. MiCA is Europe deciding to host them on its own terms, which is a far more realistic ambition than out-competing them with a capped central bank token.

Two things stand out. Neither new option pays interest, so neither is a place for savings. And the digital euro’s holding limit means that under the Commission’s proposal, a payment that would take you over it is topped up from, or spills into, a bank account you link to it. It’s designed to sit next to your bank account, not to replace it.

If you live in an EU country outside the euro, it’s less simple. The Commission’s proposal lets people there use the digital euro only once the ECB and their own central bank have agreed terms, and the final law may change that.

That’s me. I live in Bucharest and keep my savings in euros and dollars, in a savings account. So the digital euro may not be for me at all, at least at first. A euro stablecoin is available to me today, but it pays nothing and has no deposit guarantee. For saving, neither beats the account I already have. For paying, I’d take a European option at the till over another card scheme, if my bank offered one.

What to watch

  • The deal. It slipped on 18 November. Watch 3 and 16 December, the dates suggested for the next rounds: the Council wants it done before the end of the year. A deal would then need formal votes in Parliament and among governments.
  • Merchant fees. Whether Parliament’s “no worse off” rule survives France’s objection decides whether shops want it or merely have to take it.
  • The holding limit. Who sets it (the governments want that power, on the ECB’s advice), and whether it’s near €3,000. A much higher figure means the stablecoin argument won inside the ECB.
  • Qivalis. Its white paper, the token’s name, and whether it trades on the big exchanges from day one.
  • The ECB itself. Christine Lagarde leaves on 7 February 2027. The ECB says that won’t delay the project, and the pilot is due to start in the second half of 2027.

Where I land

Build it, and sell it as what it is: Europe’s own way to pay in shops and online, public, free for people and cheap for merchants, so that a payment in Lisbon or Vienna doesn’t have to cross the Atlantic. That’s a good enough reason on its own, and it’s one the ECB can actually deliver.

Leave the stablecoin fight to the tools built for it: MiCA, private euro tokens and Pontes. Judge those by whether euro tokens grow into a real market, not by whether the digital euro beats Tether.

What would change my mind? If dollar stablecoins start turning up at European tills in real amounts, through cards or wallets, then the two fights really are one, and I’d want a higher holding limit, not a lower one. And if Qivalis launches and goes nowhere, private euro tokens may not be enough, and the ECB’s case gets much stronger.
