On 29 October, Christine Lagarde announced two decisions. The first was the European Central Bank’s: it kept its deposit rate at 2.50%, after raising it in June and September. The Governing Council had debated a third increase, and she left little doubt that one is coming in December. The second decision was her own. She’ll step down after the meeting on 4 February 2027, about nine months early, because, in her words, “I don’t think that I can remain neutral.”

The last ECB president to raise rates into an oil shock and then leave was Jean-Claude Trichet. He raised them in April and July 2011, and left on 31 October. His successor, Mario Draghi, cut them at his first meeting, three days later.

That isn’t a forecast. But it raises the question this piece is about. The ECB has raised rates into an energy shock three times before this year. Twice, it was cutting again within four months. Once, the hikes stuck. What decided which way it went, and which of the three does 2026 look like?

The short version

  • Now: the ECB’s deposit rate is 2.50% after hikes in June and September. The next decision is on 17 December, and Lagarde chairs only one more after it. Its chief economist says a third hike would be “prudent” if nothing changes.
  • History: in 2008 and 2011 the ECB reversed its oil-shock hikes within 97 and 119 days. In 2022–23 it didn’t: the first cut came nine months after the last hike.
  • The difference: something broke in 2008 (Lehman Brothers) and in 2011 (the euro debt crisis). In 2022 nothing broke, and inflation came down.
  • The clue for 2026: core inflation was 2.5% in October, unchanged from September. That’s higher than in 2008 or 2011, but far below 2023’s peak of 5.7%.
  • Your money: cash in the bank still earns less than inflation, variable-rate mortgages in Finland, the Baltics and Portugal reprice first, and most household gas bills haven’t caught up with wholesale prices.

Three hikes into expensive energy

Before this year, the ECB had raised rates into an energy shock three times: in 2008, in 2011 and in 2022. Each time it gave almost the same reason: dearer energy mustn’t turn into dearer everything. The endings couldn’t have been more different, and the table at the end of this section puts them side by side.

2008: the hike at the top

On 3 July 2008 the ECB raised its main rate from 4.00% to 4.25%. That day, Brent closed at $143.95 a barrel, the highest price in the US Energy Information Administration’s daily records, which go back to 1987. The latest figures showed euro area inflation at 4.0%, with energy prices 16% higher than a year earlier. Core inflation, which leaves out energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, was 1.8%.

Trichet’s case was prevention: stop dear energy from feeding into wages and the prices of everything else. Lehman Brothers failed ten weeks later. On 8 October, the ECB cut by half a point, together with the Federal Reserve, the Bank of England and three other central banks. It was 97 days after the hike. By May 2009 the rate was 1.00%, 3.25 points below the July peak.

2011: the hikes that didn’t survive the handover

Trichet raised rates again on 7 April 2011, from 1.00% to 1.25%, and on 7 July, to 1.50%. The reasons were the same: energy and food were pushing inflation up, to about 2.7%, and the ECB wanted to stop it spreading. Core inflation was 1.2–1.6%.

Draghi took over on 1 November and cut on 3 November, while headline inflation was still 3.0%. The ECB expected it to fall below 2% during 2012 as the economy weakened. He cut again in December, and both hikes were gone. It was 119 days after the July hike.

The ECB itself has argued over this one. In 2018 its vice-president, Vítor Constâncio, said that with hindsight the hikes had been premature, but that they didn’t cause the 2012–13 recession: he blamed governments’ budget cuts and the sovereign debt crisis. Two ECB economists, Philipp Hartmann and Frank Smets, found the hikes defensible on the forecasts the ECB had at the time.

2022–23: the hikes that stuck

On 21 July 2022 the ECB raised rates for the first time in 11 years, by half a point. Nine more hikes followed, and by September 2023 the deposit rate had gone from −0.50% to 4.00%. Inflation peaked at 10.6% in October 2022, with energy up 41.7%. Core inflation kept rising after that, to a record 5.7% in March 2023.

Nothing broke. Inflation came down, and the first cut came on 6 June 2024, nine months after the last hike and almost two years after the first.

What forced the U-turns

It’s tempting to read 2008 and 2011 as proof that hiking into an oil shock is a mistake. The record doesn’t show that. In both years the ECB reversed course because something else broke: the banking system in 2008, government debt markets in 2011. The ECB’s own announcements say so.

What the hikes did do was leave the ECB tightening just as demand was about to collapse. An oil shock is already a tax on everyone who buys energy, paid to the countries that sell it. Add a crisis, and the hikes look like a mistake. If nothing breaks, as in 2022, they look like insurance that paid off.

So the question for 2026 isn’t whether the ECB will repeat 2008. It’s whether something is about to break, and whether energy is spreading into everything else.

My read: 2026 looks most like 2011. Not because of oil, but because of France. In 2011 the ECB raised rates and then watched government bond markets crack. This year, France’s borrowing costs are further above Germany’s than at any time since that crisis, and the ECB is talking about a third hike anyway. Core inflation is higher than it was in 2011, and that’s the one thing keeping 2022 on the table. But nothing in the wage data says we’re heading there.

The case for hiking anyway

The best argument for the hikes is the 1970s. After the oil shocks of 1973 and 1979, inflation became entrenched in the countries where wages chased prices, and stayed far lower in West Germany, where the Bundesbank had already tightened before the first shock and did so again in 1979–80.

Interest rates weren’t the only difference. Italy’s scala mobile linked wages to prices automatically, and Britain’s governments tried to fix inflation with wage and price controls rather than monetary policy. German wages rose fast too, by 12% in 1973. But the lesson the ECB took is that waiting for a wage spiral to appear means acting after it has started.

It has written that lesson into its rules. After the 2021–23 surge, the ECB’s 2025 strategy review concluded that it needed to react to big shocks in both directions. In June, Lagarde set out three cases for the European Parliament:

  • A small, temporary shock: look through it.
  • A sizeable shock that isn’t too persistent: a measured adjustment. She put 2026 here.
  • A significant, persistent shock: a forceful response, to stop prices and wages feeding each other.

Isabel Schnabel, a member of the ECB’s Executive Board, defended both hikes in September, and not only because of oil: she also pointed to government spending and leftover price pressure at home.

I think the ECB got one of the two right. June was a mistake. Brent was falling on the day the ECB raised rates, and three weeks later it was cheaper than before the war. That’s Trichet in July 2008: hiking on the way down. September was different. The second spike was real: Brent went from $96 to $121 in the first ten days of the month, and core inflation was already above where it had been in 2008 or 2011. If I’d had a vote, I’d have said no in June and yes in September.

In a way, the second spike bailed out the first hike. What would change my mind about June: core inflation climbing towards 3% this winter. Then June looks early, not wrong.

What’s different in 2026

On the surface, 2026 looks like the earlier episodes: oil above $120 for most of October, energy inflation above 20% and an ECB that has raised rates twice. It isn’t alone this time, either. On 5 November the Bank of England raised its rate by a quarter point, to 4%, to stop dearer energy feeding into other prices, and one of its nine members wanted half a point. Look closer, though, and six things don’t fit the pattern. Some of them argue for more hikes, and some for stopping.

The oil price has done something odd

The war began on 28 February, and Iran shut the Strait of Hormuz, which carries about a fifth of the world’s seaborne oil. The International Energy Agency called it the largest supply disruption in the oil market’s history. Brent went from $71 to $138 by 7 April.

Then it fell. On 11 June, the day of the first hike, it was $92.84 and dropping, and three weeks later it was $68.53, cheaper than before the war. That’s 2008 all over again: a hike, then a collapse in the price that prompted it. But the shock came back. On 10 September, the day of the second hike, Brent was $120.98, and by 2 October it was $135.51.

And now it’s falling again. In the first week of November Brent lost 16%, its steepest weekly fall since the start of the Covid pandemic, as traders bet on talks to end the war. On 10 November it briefly dipped below $90 for the first time since August. A pause in the strikes isn’t a peace deal, though, and this year the price has already fallen once and come back.

Core inflation is higher

Headline inflation rose to 4.0% in October, according to Eurostat’s flash estimate, and energy inflation to 20.8%. Core inflation stayed at 2.5% for a second month. In 2008 and 2011, the two quick reversals, it was below 2%. That’s the strongest reason not to read 2026 as a rerun of either. It’s still far below 2023, when the hikes stuck.

Wages haven’t moved, but households are uneasy

Pay per employee grew 3.3% a year in the second quarter, down from 3.6%. The ECB’s wage tracker points to agreed pay rises of 2.5–2.8% for the rest of the year, and the ECB says wages show no material response so far.

Households are less calm. In the ECB’s September consumer survey, the typical person expected inflation of 2.9% three years from now. That’s up from 2.6% in January, before the war, so the shift is real but smaller than it looks. The same people expect the economy to shrink by 1.5% and unemployment to rise. Higher prices and fewer jobs, at the same time: that’s what stagflation looks like in a survey.

It’s becoming a gas shock

Wholesale gas briefly touched €100 a megawatt-hour on 28 October, more than three times its level at the start of the year. It has eased since, to about €87 on 9 November, but EU storage is only about 74% full, against about 83% a year ago. Most household bills haven’t followed yet: in Germany, household gas was 2.8% cheaper in September than a year earlier. Fixed-price contracts and regulated tariffs delay the rise, so if wholesale prices stay this high, more of it is likely to reach bills as contracts renew. That makes this year closer to 2022 than to 2008.

Bond markets are tightening for the ECB

Ten-year government bond yields in the euro area have risen by about 0.35 points on average since September, the ECB says, and it now counts higher long-term rates as a risk that could pull inflation down. France is the extreme case: in late October its ten-year yield was about 1.6 points above Germany’s, the widest gap since the euro crisis, and in November it’s still about 1.5, as investors wait for the 2027 presidential election. ING’s chief economist, Carsten Brzeski, argues that without a French budget fix, the bond market has taken over the ECB’s job. That’s the 2011 echo: sovereign stress, arriving before the third hike rather than after the second.

Governments are pushing the other way

Germany is cutting fuel tax by 17 cents a litre from October to the end of the year, at a cost of €2.5 billion. Spain has a €5 billion package, and France is paying €100 each to 5.5 million long-distance commuters. Each is a reasonable response to a painful bill. Together, they keep demand for fuel up while the ECB tries to bring it down, which is one reason Schnabel gives for hiking.

And money is still cheap. Even after two hikes, the deposit rate is 1.5 points below inflation, the loosest of the four episodes by that rough measure. Against core inflation, it’s about neutral.

On fuel tax cuts, I’m with the hawks, which surprises me. Help for people who have to drive is fair. But a 17-cent cut on every litre sold helps the nurse on a night shift and the weekend SUV equally, and it keeps demand for fuel up at the very moment the ECB is trying to bring it down. Berlin cuts the price of diesel, Frankfurt raises the price of money, and the same household pays for both. France’s cash for long-distance commuters is the better model.

And December? If I had a vote, I’d hold at 2.50%. Someone in an Australian finance thread put it more simply than any central banker this year: expensive fuel already does what a rate hike does. In Europe, the bond market is doing it too, banks are turning down more mortgage applications, and households expect the economy to shrink. Wages haven’t moved. A third hike would squeeze an economy that’s already being squeezed from three sides. The number that would have changed my mind was October’s core inflation. It stayed at 2.5%, and oil has fallen by almost a quarter since the end of October. If anything, the case for holding has got stronger.

A president on her way out

Lagarde will chair two more decisions: 17 December and 4 February. Her successor’s first scheduled decision is 18 March 2027, if one has been appointed by then. No successor has been named.

The Trichet parallel is neat, but don’t lean on it too hard. Draghi didn’t cut in 2011 because he was new. He cut because the debt crisis had wrecked the outlook, and the ECB decides by committee. Lagarde stressed continuity on 29 October. What the handover does is put a date on the question: by March, someone else will have to decide whether this year’s hikes were 2011 or 2022.

Markets expect one more, and so, it seems, does the ECB’s chief economist. On 10 November Philip Lane told Bloomberg Television that if the information the ECB has in December is the same as today, “then I think it would be prudent indeed to hike”, Reuters reported. That week, market pricing put the odds of a quarter-point rise in December at about 80%.

I don’t mind Lagarde wanting to speak her mind. I mind the timing. She has told us she can’t stay neutral, and she’ll chair two more decisions, with France’s election next spring and French bonds under the most pressure since the euro crisis. Everything she says in December will be read through that. And if the ECB hikes on 17 December, her successor inherits a rate path set by someone who was already halfway out of the door. The ECB is a committee, and Lagarde says nothing will change. I believe the first part.

Through your wallet

A rate decision in Frankfurt is one number. By the time it reaches a household it has become several: a mortgage payment, the interest on a savings account, a heating bill. Which of them you feel first depends mostly on where you live.

Most of Portugal’s new loans are mixed, fixed for a few years and then variable, and a German “fixed” rate usually lasts about 10 years. Spain still has about 45% of its outstanding balances on a floating rate, although almost 80% of new lending is fixed for the long term.

Portugal, Germany and Spain: European Mortgage Federation, Hypostat 2026.

Twelve-month Euribor was 3.29% on 28 October, against 2.20% a year earlier. On a €200,000 variable mortgage over 25 years, a rise of 1.1 points adds about €120 to the monthly payment. Banks are also getting stricter: in the ECB’s October lending survey, demand for mortgages fell again in the third quarter, more applications were rejected, and banks expect to tighten their rules in the fourth.

I rent in Bucharest, and my rent has been flat for two years, so none of this has reached me through housing. What struck me reading European mortgage threads this year is how little we know about each other’s systems. In one, someone asked, genuinely surprised, whether other Europeans really get fixed rates for more than five years. Same currency, same central bank, and the same decision is a pay cut in Helsinki and a non-event in Lyon.

The fixed-rate countries aren’t off the hook, though. Another commenter in that thread made the point I can’t shake: when most borrowers are shielded, the ECB has to push harder to have any effect, and the cost lands on whoever isn’t. That means first-time buyers, variable-rate borrowers, and people like the Irish borrower who described themselves as a mortgage prisoner: never a missed payment, and still no bank willing to let them switch. If I were choosing a mortgage now, I’d pay up for a longer fix. Not because I know where rates are going, but because this year has shown how little anyone does.

Your savings

The average overnight deposit in the euro area paid 0.30% in September, according to the ECB’s provisional figures. New term deposits of up to a year paid 2.33%. Inflation was 3.8%. Two hikes haven’t changed the basic sum: cash in a current account loses about 3.5% of its value a year, and a typical one-year deposit loses about 1.5%.

My cash sits in a savings account, in euros and dollars. With the leu at a record low against the euro last month, I’m glad it isn’t in lei. But the overnight rate is the number that annoys me most. Two hikes in, the average euro area current account pays 0.30%. Someone in an Irish personal finance thread put it bluntly: cheap mortgages are paid for by billions sitting in accounts that earn nothing. I can’t measure that, but the gap between what banks pay savers and what they charge borrowers isn’t hard to see. If your bank pays you almost nothing, you’re its cheapest source of money. Check what yours actually pays before assuming it passed the hikes on.

Your energy bills

An oil shock doesn’t raise every bill at once. In Germany in September, petrol and diesel were 37.1% dearer than a year earlier and heating oil 74.9%, while electricity was 5.5% cheaper and household gas 2.8% cheaper. In Austria, heating oil was up 63.1%, gas up 11.8% and electricity down 9.3%. If you heat with oil or drive a lot, you’ve felt this shock. If you heat with gas, you may not have yet.

Fuel is where I feel it. Filling up with 98-octane now costs me about €120. Two or three years ago, premium petrol in Bucharest was around 7.8 lei a litre. At the end of October the cheapest in the city was 10.59 lei, more than a third higher. It’s the bill I see every week, and it feels like a lot more than a third.

The supermarket is the same story, told differently. I stopped shopping at Kaufland and moved to Lidl. My usual basket there costs €50–60, about what I used to spend at Kaufland two or three years ago, when I bought the branded stuff rather than the own label. So my bill looks the same as it did. What’s in the bag doesn’t. An inflation index keeps pricing the same basket; it doesn’t see me changing shops and brands to keep the total flat.

Outside the euro

Romania had the highest inflation in the EU in September, at 6.5% on the EU measure. Its central bank has kept its main rate at 6.50% since August 2024 and expects fuel and energy to push inflation up again by the end of the year. Poland was at 4.0%, Czechia 2.2%, Hungary 2.1% and Sweden 1.0%. Outside the euro, the ECB still reaches you through the exchange rate and any loan you have in euros.

What if you’d bought oil?

Every oil spike brings the same question: should I have bought some? Try it below. Set the start to July 2008, the month of Trichet’s hike. In dollars, $1,000 was worth about $761 on 3 November, 18 years later, even after this year’s spike. At the Covid low in April 2020, it was worth $106. Switch to euros and €1,000 became about €1,074, because a euro bought $1.58 then and $1.12 now. Adjust for inflation and both are losses. The calculator uses the latest weekly prices, so these figures move as new ones come in.

Then try February 2026, the month before the war, and see how much difference a few weeks make.

Gold, the other classic hedge, has had the opposite year. It hit a record of $5,594 an ounce on 29 January and was about $4,038 on 29 October, 28% lower, partly because rising interest rates make an asset that pays nothing less attractive.

My honest view: most people shouldn’t try to hedge an energy shock by buying energy. Play with the calculator and the problem is obvious. Buy at the wrong moment and you’re still underwater 18 years later, and the funds you could actually buy did far worse than the price on the chart. Gold was supposed to be the safe one, and at the end of October it was 28% below its January record.

I’m risk averse, so what I own is boring on purpose: the S&P 500, the housing market and a small percentage of Bitcoin. Bitcoin isn’t a hedge against inflation, whatever you’ve been told. It works only if you really know what you’re doing and won’t panic-sell in the next 50% drop. Most traditional investors aren’t built for that kind of volatility, which is why they mostly stay away. On returns, though, it’s still one of the best assets you can hold, and that’s why I keep a slice. The rest of my hedge is the dull part: a cash buffer, and never being forced to sell anything at the bottom.

Disclosure: I write for Bitcoin.com and own a small amount of Bitcoin.

What to watch before 17 December

  • 18 November, full October inflation: whether Eurostat confirms the flash estimate, and the figures for countries outside the euro, Romania included.
  • 27 November, the ECB’s consumer survey: whether households’ three-year expectation moves beyond 2.9%.
  • 1 December, November inflation: the last reading before the decision. Core inflation is the number that matters. If it climbs above 2.5%, the 2022 comparison gets stronger.
  • Oil: whether November’s fall lasts. A deal over the Strait of Hormuz would take much of the pressure off; a collapse in the talks would bring it back.
  • Wages: any sign that pay deals are catching up with prices. So far there’s none.
  • Gas and storage: a cold winter with storage at 74% would turn this into 2022.
  • France: if its borrowing costs keep rising, the bond market keeps doing the ECB’s job, and the case for a December hike weakens.
  • 17 December: the decision, with the ECB’s new forecasts. If they show inflation back at 2% without another hike, that’s the argument for stopping.

If you watch one number, make it core inflation on 1 December. Oil gets the headlines, but oil is the part the ECB can’t do anything about. Core inflation tells you whether dear energy is turning into dear everything. October’s held at 2.5%. If November’s holds or falls, there’s no case for a December hike, whatever Lane says. If it jumps, I’ll have to eat some of what I’ve written here. And if you have room for a second number, watch the gap between French and German ten-year yields. That’s where 2011 would start.

Where I land

June was a mistake, September was defensible, and December should be a hold. Of the three earlier episodes, 2026 looks most like 2011: a central bank tightening into a supply shock while a bond market cracks under it, with a president on the way out. The difference is core inflation, which is high enough that I could be wrong. If I am, it’ll be because core goes up, not because oil does.

What I’m sure of is the lesson of 2008 and 2011. The hikes themselves weren’t what went wrong. What went wrong was hiking just before something else broke. The ECB can’t know what will break next. But it can notice that the bond market is already doing its job.
