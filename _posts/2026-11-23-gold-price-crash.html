On 30 January, Donald Trump said he would nominate Kevin Warsh to chair the Federal Reserve. Gold had set a record two days earlier, at $5,307 an ounce. That Friday it fell by about a tenth, its worst day since the early 1980s. Silver lost more than a third.

What came next should have been gold’s year. On 28 February war broke out in the Gulf, and Iran shut the Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude went from $71 a barrel to $138 by 7 April. Inflation reached 4.0% in the euro area and 3.7% in America. War, an oil shock and inflation are the three things gold is supposed to love. Instead, its monthly average price fell in seven of the eight months after the war began. On 10 November, the day US consumer prices for October came in hotter than expected, gold fell about 2%. On Friday 20 November it ended its worst week in eight months near $3,540, a third below January’s record.

Gold has been here before. Since 1971, when the dollar’s last link to gold was cut, it has crashed from a record high three times. After one, it was back at its peak in three years. After another, it took 26. What decided which, and which one is 2026?

The short version

  • Now: gold ended last week near $3,540 an ounce, 33% below its record of $5,307 on 28 January and almost a fifth lower than at the start of the year. In euros the fall is smaller, about 28%, because the dollar has risen.
  • History: since 1971 gold has crashed from a record three times: from December 1974, January 1980 and September 2011. On monthly averages it was back at the peak after three years, 26 years and nine years.
  • The difference: real interest rates, and who was selling. Gold pays nothing, so it loses when safe bonds pay well above inflation. After 1980 they did for two decades, while Europe’s central banks sold their gold.
  • 2026: the yield on ten-year inflation-protected US bonds is 2.88%, near its highest since 2008, and the Fed is expected to raise rates again on 9 December. But central banks are buying gold, not selling it.
  • Your money: gold pays no interest, and its long slumps are measured in years. Someone who bought at January 1980’s average waited until 2006 to get their dollars back, and until 2025 to beat US inflation.

Three crashes from a record

Gold only became a market price in the 1970s. The US had fixed it at $35 an ounce since 1934, and Richard Nixon ended the dollar’s convertibility into gold in August 1971. Each of the three crashes since then started from a record, and the chart lines them up from the month of the peak.

1974–76: the first free market

For 41 years Americans weren’t allowed to own gold bullion. The ban ended on 31 December 1974, and the price was close to $200 an ounce as the day approached, more than five times the old official price. Then the sellers came instead of the buyers. The US Treasury auctioned part of its stock in 1975, and the International Monetary Fund agreed to sell a sixth of its gold, 25 million ounces, starting in June 1976. By August 1976 the monthly average was $110, 40% below December 1974’s.

The fall didn’t last, because the Fed let it go. Interest rates had briefly risen above inflation in 1976, but inflation came back, and by late 1978 a ten-year Treasury paid no more than prices were rising. By March 1978 gold was back at December 1974’s average. By January 1980 it had nearly quadrupled again.

1980–82: the Volcker crash

The 1970s ended in a panic. US inflation was 13.9% in January 1980, the Soviet Union had just invaded Afghanistan, and on 21 January gold touched $850, a record that stood until 2008. Paul Volcker had become chair of the Fed the previous August, and in October 1979 he changed how it fought inflation. The federal funds rate averaged 19% in January 1981 and again in June.

What mattered for gold was that rates rose above inflation and stayed there. In January 1980 a ten-year Treasury yielded about 3 points less than inflation; by June 1982 it yielded about 7 points more. That month gold’s average was $315, 53% below January 1980’s.

It took 26 years to get back. The monthly average didn’t pass January 1980’s until May 2006, and after US inflation it took until February 2025. In today’s dollars, the $850 peak is worth about $3,680, so at $3,540, gold is once again below its 1980 high.

2011–15: the taper crash

The third crash followed the financial crisis. With the Fed’s rate at zero after two rounds of bond buying, and real yields near zero, gold set a record above $1,920 an ounce on 6 September 2011. Then the Fed began to talk about stopping. In April 2013 gold lost 13% in two trading days, and in May Ben Bernanke said the Fed could slow its bond buying within months. The ten-year real yield went from −0.87% in December 2012 to +0.92% in September 2013.

By December 2015, the month the Fed raised rates for the first time since 2006, gold’s monthly average was $1,076, 39% below September 2011’s. It was back in July 2020, nine years after the peak, when real yields had gone below zero again during the pandemic.

What decided how long

Gold pays nothing: no coupon, no dividend, no rent. Holding it means giving up whatever a safe bond would have paid. When that bond pays less than inflation, as it did at all three peaks, giving it up costs nothing in real terms. When it pays well above inflation, every year in gold is a year of real income lost. Edward Chancellor, whose book I reviewed this month, calls interest the price of time. Gold is what people buy when time is cheap.

That fits all three crashes. Each began with real interest rates at or below zero and gathered pace as they rose, and each ended only when rates fell back. In 1977–78 that happened within two years. After 2011 it took until 2019, when the Fed started cutting again.

After 1980, real rates were only half the story. The other half is that central banks, many of them in Europe, spent two decades selling their gold. The World Gold Council counts 3,554 tonnes sold between 1989 and 1999 alone. On 7 May 1999 Gordon Brown announced that Britain would sell more than half of its gold. The price fell 10% before the first auction, and on 20 July it touched $252.80, a 20-year low. Britain went on to sell 395 tonnes at an average of about $275 an ounce.

Europe then called time on it. On 26 September 1999 the ECB and 14 other European central banks, the Bank of England among them, agreed to cap their sales at about 400 tonnes a year for five years. Gold jumped from about $260 to about $330 in two weeks. July 1999 turned out to be the bottom: gold’s monthly average has never been lower since.

The rule that broke, and came back

For two decades, the rule was simple enough to draw on one chart. When the yield on ten-year inflation-protected US bonds went up, gold went down, and the other way round. By my calculation on monthly figures, from 2003 to 2021 the correlation between gold in today’s dollars and that real yield was −0.82: close to a mirror image.

Then it broke. From 2022 the Fed raised rates faster than at any time since the 1980s, and the real yield went from about −1% to about 2.5% by October 2023. On the old rule, gold should have collapsed. Instead it rose, from about $1,800 at the start of 2022 to $5,020 in February 2026. From 2022 to October 2026, the correlation was +0.49.

The reason was a new kind of buyer. After Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, Western governments froze about $300bn of its central bank’s reserves. Central banks elsewhere drew the obvious conclusion: dollars and euros held abroad can be frozen, gold in your own vault can’t. They bought more than 1,000 tonnes in each of 2022, 2023 and 2024. They don’t buy because of what Treasuries pay, so for three years, real yields stopped mattering.

This year they started to matter again. The real yield rose from 1.72% on 27 February to 2.95% on 5 October, its highest since November 2008, and was 2.88% on 19 November. Gold has fallen almost every step of the way.

What’s different in 2026

Four things make this crash unlike the other three. Two argue that it has further to run, and two that it won’t become 1980.

The Fed is raising rates into an oil shock

Under Warsh, the Fed raised its rate on 16 September for the first time since 2023, to 3.75–4.00%. In October two officials voted for another hike. After October’s inflation figures, Governor Christopher Waller raised the possibility of half a point in December, and by 20 November markets put the chance of at least a quarter-point rise on 9 December at 96%. The ten-year Treasury yield reached 5.31% on 5 October, its highest since 2002. That’s the 1980 ingredient: a central bank willing to push rates above inflation while prices are still rising.

The debasement trade has lost its premise

Much of gold’s rise in 2025 rested on a bet that the Fed would bend to the White House and let inflation run. Trump has said US rates should be “1% or less”, and the market read his choice of chair as the test. Warsh has so far raised rates in defiance of him. Gold’s worst day in four decades was the day he was named.

Forced sellers

November’s fall wasn’t only about the Fed. As technology shares slid, some investors sold gold to cover losses elsewhere, which one trader described to Reuters as forced liquidations. Gold is easy to sell in a margin call, which is one reason it often falls at the start of a market panic. And there is plenty to sell: holdings in gold funds reached a record 4,256 tonnes at the end of September. Between 2013 and 2015, funds sold 1,215 tonnes, and that selling made the last crash longer.

Central banks are still buying

This is what 1980 didn’t have. Central banks bought a net 321 tonnes in the third quarter, 42% more than a year earlier and the most since the end of 2024. In September alone they bought 61 tonnes, the most in any month this year, with Poland and China leading. Poland’s central bank, Europe’s biggest buyer, is doing the opposite of what Western Europe’s did in the 1990s.

So the two big forces point in opposite directions. Real rates say this crash has room to run. The central banks say there’s a buyer underneath it that wasn’t there in 1980 or in 2013.

Through your wallet

Gold is priced in dollars, so for a saver in Europe the exchange rate matters as much as the metal. On 28 January, gold’s record was €4,444 an ounce. At Friday’s price and the ECB’s reference rate of $1.0990, it was about €3,220: down 28%, against 33% in dollars. The dollar’s rise since then (the euro bought $1.1974 on 28 January) has cushioned the fall for anyone who counts in euros.

I keep part of my savings in dollars, so I’ve had the other side of that move: since the start of the year a dollar has gone from about 85 euro cents to about 91.

What you give up to hold gold depends on where you live. In America a ten-year Treasury now pays about 2.9 points more than expected inflation, the main reason gold is falling. In the euro area, money is still cheap in real terms: the ECB’s deposit rate is 2.50% and a ten-year German Bund yields 3.39%, both below October’s 4.0% inflation. But gold’s price is set in dollars, by American real rates, so European savers get the fall without the higher real yields that cause it.

Three things worth knowing before buying gold in the EU:

  • No VAT on investment gold. EU law exempts bars at least 99.5% pure and coins at least 90% pure that were minted after 1800 and are or were legal tender (VAT Directive, articles 344 to 356). Silver bars and jewellery carry VAT.
  • The spread is the real fee. Dealers sell coins and small bars above the metal’s price and buy them back below it, and the gap is widest on the smallest pieces. Exchange-traded products backed by gold in a vault cost less to trade, but charge a yearly fee; check that the one you’re looking at holds metal rather than futures.
  • The long slumps are real. Someone who bought at January 1980’s average waited until 2006 to get their dollars back. The site’s old-versus-new calculator shows what gold did in euros since 1999, after inflation, against cash, deposits, Bunds and shares.

What to watch

  • The Fed on 9 December. A quarter point is priced in. Half a point would tell markets that Warsh means what he says.
  • US inflation for November, on 10 December, the day after the decision. October’s annual rate couldn’t be published, because the government shutdown left no figure for October 2025, so this is the first clean reading since September’s 3.7%.
  • The ten-year real yield. Above 3% would be its highest since the 2008 crash. Below 2.5% would mean markets think the hiking is nearly done.
  • Central bank buying. The World Gold Council’s monthly figures, and its full-year report early next year. If the buying slows while real yields stay high, that’s the 1980s mix.
  • The Strait of Hormuz. Oddly, a peace deal could be good for gold: cheaper oil means lower inflation, and fewer reasons for the Fed to keep raising rates.
  • The ECB on 17 December. Its decision moves the euro, and so the price of gold in euros.

Where I land

Gold isn’t an inflation hedge, and 2026 is the proof: inflation went to 4% on both sides of the Atlantic, and gold lost a third. It’s a bet against real interest rates, and on fear. When a central bank falls behind inflation, gold wins. When it gets ahead, gold loses, however high inflation is.

Which crash is 2026? My read is that it looks more like 2011 than 1980. Not 1980, because real yields of 2.9% are high but nowhere near the 7 points Volcker reached, and because this time the biggest buyers, central banks, buy for reasons that have nothing to do with yields. In 1980 central banks weren’t buying, and from the late 1980s they sold. Not 1976 either, because the Fed is still raising rates, and a chair who began by defying the White House has every reason to keep proving it.

What would change my mind: real yields staying near 3% through 2027 while central banks slow their buying. That’s the 1980s mix, and then the wait is measured in decades. The other way round, a peace deal, falling oil and a Fed that cuts while inflation is still above 2% would look like 1977.
