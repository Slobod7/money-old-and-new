Before you read too much into this: it uses the spot price, for whole cargoes of physical oil, which no ordinary investor could buy and keep. Funds that track oil futures earned far less than the spot price, because rolling from one contract to the next usually costs money. USO, a US fund that holds oil futures, shows the gap. From its launch on 10 April 2006 to the end of 2025, its value per share fell 87%. The spot price of WTI, the US crude it tracks, fell 16%. This is history, not a forecast or advice.

How it works

  • Each purchase is at EIA’s Brent spot price on the first trading day of the month. A lump sum buys once, in the start month.
  • Fractions of a barrel are allowed.
  • Today’s value uses the latest daily price: ${{ ex.latest_price }} on {{ cc_to }}.
  • In euros, each purchase converts at the ECB’s rate for that day (or its last rate before, on days it published none), and the value converts back at its rate for {{ cc_to }}.
  • Adjusting for inflation uses US consumer prices (CPI, to {{ ba.cpi_to | append: "-01" | date: "%B %Y" }}) for dollars and euro area consumer prices (HICP, to {{ ba.hicp_to | append: "-01" | date: "%B %Y" }}) for euros. Later months count as today’s money.{% for m in ba.cpi_filled %} US CPI for {{ m | append: "-01" | date: "%B %Y" }} was never published, so it’s taken as halfway between the months either side.{% endfor %}{% for m in ba.hicp_filled %} HICP for {{ m | append: "-01" | date: "%B %Y" }} is taken as halfway between the months either side.{% endfor %}
  • Fees, taxes and storage aren’t included.

Sources: U.S. Energy Information Administration (Brent spot price); European Central Bank (euro reference rates, and euro area HICP from Eurostat); U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics consumer prices, via FRED. Prices to {{ cc_to }}. Updated {{ ba.updated | date: "%-d %B %Y" }}.
